Chapter 4: Carbon and its Compounds

This chapter introduces carbon, an element of immense significance, both in its elemental and combined forms. Many everyday items, from food and clothes to medicines and books, are based on this versatile element. All living structures are also carbon-based. Despite its crucial role, the amount of carbon in the Earth's crust (0.02% as minerals like carbonates, hydrogen-carbonates, coal, petroleum) and atmosphere (0.03% as carbon dioxide) is quite small.

4.1 Bonding in Carbon – The Covalent Bond

  • Properties of Carbon Compounds
  • Unlike ionic compounds which have high melting and boiling points and conduct electricity in solution or molten state, most carbon compounds are poor conductors of electricity.
  • Carbon compounds generally have low melting and boiling points compared to ionic compounds.
  • This suggests that the forces of attraction between molecules in carbon compounds are not very strong.
  • Since they are largely non-conductors, bonding in these compounds does not produce ions.
  • Electronic Configuration of Carbon
  • The atomic number of carbon is 6.
  • It has four electrons in its outermost shell (valence electrons).
  • To achieve a stable noble gas configuration (a completely filled outer shell), carbon needs to either gain or lose four electrons.
  • Challenges of gaining/losing electrons:
  • Gaining four electrons to form a C⁴⁻ anion would be difficult for its nucleus (with 6 protons) to hold on to ten electrons (four extra electrons).
  • Losing four electrons to form a C⁴⁺ cation would require a very large amount of energy, leaving a carbon cation with 6 protons holding only two electrons.
  • Covalent Bonding: Sharing Electrons
  • Carbon overcomes this problem by sharing its valence electrons with other carbon atoms or atoms of other elements.
  • Many other elements also form molecules by sharing electrons.
  • The shared electrons are considered to belong to the outermost shells of both atoms, allowing both to attain a noble gas configuration.
  • Covalent bonds are formed by the sharing of an electron pair between two atoms.
  • Examples of Covalent Bonds:
  • Hydrogen (H₂): Atomic number 1. Each hydrogen atom has one electron in its K shell and needs one more. Two hydrogen atoms share their electrons to form an H₂ molecule, allowing each to attain the configuration of helium (2 electrons in K shell). This is a single covalent bond.
  • Oxygen (O₂): Atomic number 8. Each oxygen atom has six electrons in its L shell and needs two more. Each atom shares two electrons with another oxygen atom, forming two shared pairs of electrons. This constitutes a double bond.
  • Nitrogen (N₂): Atomic number 7. Each nitrogen atom needs three electrons to complete its octet. In an N₂ molecule, each nitrogen atom contributes three electrons, forming three shared pairs. This constitutes a triple bond.
  • Methane (CH₄): A simple carbon compound used as a fuel. Carbon is tetravalent (has four valence electrons). It shares these four electrons with four hydrogen atoms (each with valency 1) to achieve a noble gas configuration.
  • Properties of Covalently Bonded Molecules:
  • They have strong bonds within the molecule but weak intermolecular forces.
  • This results in their low melting and boiling points.
  • Since electrons are shared and no charged particles (ions) are formed, covalent compounds are generally poor conductors of electricity.
  • Allotropes of Carbon
  • Carbon exists in nature in different forms with widely varying physical properties, known as allotropes.
  • Diamond: Each carbon atom is bonded to four other carbon atoms, forming a rigid three-dimensional structure. It is the hardest substance known. Synthetic diamonds can be made under high pressure and temperature.
  • Graphite: Each carbon atom is bonded to three other carbon atoms in the same plane, forming a hexagonal array. One of these bonds is a double bond, satisfying carbon's valency. The hexagonal arrays are arranged in layers one above the other. Graphite is smooth and slippery and a very good conductor of electricity.
  • Fullerenes: Another class of carbon allotropes. The first identified was C-60, with carbon atoms arranged in the shape of a football, named after Buckminster Fuller.

    • 4.2 Versatile Nature of Carbon

    The ability of carbon to form a vast number of compounds (estimated in millions, outnumbering compounds of all other elements combined) is due to its covalent bonding and two key factors:

  • Catenation:
  • Carbon has the unique ability to form bonds with other atoms of carbon, giving rise to large molecules.
  • These compounds can have long chains, branched chains, or ring structures of carbon atoms.
  • Carbon atoms can be linked by single, double, or triple bonds.
  • Saturated compounds: Compounds with only single bonds between carbon atoms.
  • Unsaturated compounds: Compounds with double or triple bonds between their carbon atoms.
  • No other element exhibits catenation to this extent. The carbon-carbon bond is very strong and stable, contributing to the large number of compounds.
  • Tetravalency:
  • Carbon has a valency of four, meaning it can bond with four other atoms.
  • It can bond with other carbon atoms or atoms of other monovalent elements.
  • Carbon forms compounds with elements like oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen, sulphur, and chlorine, resulting in compounds with specific properties.
  • Bonds formed by carbon with most other elements are also very strong and exceptionally stable. This strength is attributed to carbon's small size, which allows its nucleus to hold strongly to the shared electron pairs.
  • Organic Compounds
  • Historically, carbon compounds were thought to be formed only within living systems by a "vital force".
  • Friedrich Wöhler disproved this in 1828 by synthesizing urea from ammonium cyanate.
  • However, most carbon compounds (except carbides, oxides of carbon, carbonates, and hydrogencarbonate salts) continue to be studied under organic chemistry.

    • 4.2.1 Saturated and Unsaturated Carbon Compounds

  • Saturated Carbon Compounds:
  • Contain only single bonds between carbon atoms.
  • Examples: Methane (CH₄), Ethane (C₂H₆).
  • To draw structures, first link carbon atoms with single bonds, then satisfy remaining valencies with hydrogen atoms.
  • These compounds are normally not very reactive.
  • Unsaturated Carbon Compounds:
  • Contain double or triple bonds between carbon atoms.
  • Examples: Ethene (C₂H₄) (double bond), Ethyne (C₂H₂) (triple bond).
  • These compounds are more reactive than saturated carbon compounds.

    • 4.2.2 Chains, Branches and Rings

  • Chains: Carbon atoms can form straight chains of varying lengths, such as methane (1 carbon), ethane (2 carbons), propane (3 carbons), butane (4 carbons), pentane (5 carbons), and hexane (6 carbons).
  • Structural Isomers: Compounds with identical molecular formulas but different structures are called structural isomers. For example, butane (C₄H₁₀) can have a straight chain or a branched chain.
  • Rings: Some carbon compounds have carbon atoms arranged in a ring form. Examples include cyclohexane (C₆H₁₂) and benzene (C₆H₆).
  • Hydrocarbons: Compounds containing only carbon and hydrogen.
  • Alkanes: Saturated hydrocarbons (only single bonds).
  • Alkenes: Unsaturated hydrocarbons with one or more double bonds.
  • Alkynes: Unsaturated hydrocarbons with one or more triple bonds.

    • 4.2.3 Will you be my Friend? (Heteroatoms and Functional Groups)

  • Carbon readily forms bonds with other elements like halogens (Cl, Br), oxygen, nitrogen, and sulphur.
  • In a hydrocarbon chain, one or more hydrogen atoms can be replaced by these elements.
  • Heteroatom: An element replacing hydrogen in a hydrocarbon chain.
  • Functional Groups: These heteroatoms, or groups containing them, confer specific properties to the compound, regardless of the carbon chain's length and nature. They are attached to the carbon chain by replacing one or more hydrogen atoms.
  • Examples of Functional Groups:
  • Haloalkanes: -Cl (Chloro), -Br (Bromo)
  • Alcohol: -OH
  • Aldehyde: -CHO
  • Ketone: -CO-
  • Carboxylic acid: -COOH

    • 4.2.4 Homologous Series

  • Definition: A series of compounds in which the same functional group substitutes for hydrogen in a carbon chain.
  • Members of a homologous series exhibit very similar chemical properties because these properties are determined solely by the functional group.
  • Successive members in a homologous series differ by a –CH₂- unit. This means their molecular masses also differ by 14 u (12 u for C + 2 u for H).
  • Examples:
  • Alcohols: CH₃OH, C₂H₅OH, C₃H₇OH, C₄H₉OH.
  • Alkanes (general formula CnH₂n+₂) [inference from CnH2n for alkenes and the -CH2- difference].
  • Alkenes (general formula CnH₂n), where n = 2, 3, 4, ....
  • Alkynes (general formula CnH₂n-₂) [inference from CnH2n for alkenes and the -CH2- difference].
  • Physical Properties: As molecular mass increases within a homologous series, a gradation in physical properties is observed. Melting and boiling points increase, and solubility in specific solvents shows a similar gradation.

    • 4.2.5 Nomenclature of Carbon Compounds

    The naming of compounds in a homologous series follows a system based on the basic carbon chain, modified by a prefix or suffix indicating the functional group.

  • Steps for Naming:
  • Identify the number of carbon atoms in the compound. (e.g., three carbon atoms = propane).
  • If a functional group is present, it's indicated by a prefix or suffix.
  • If the functional group suffix begins with a vowel (a, e, i, o, u), the final ‘e’ of the carbon chain name is deleted before adding the suffix. (e.g., Propane – ‘e’ + ‘one’ (ketone) = Propanone).
  • If the carbon chain is unsaturated, the final ‘ane’ in the name is replaced by ‘ene’ (for double bonds) or ‘yne’ (for triple bonds). (e.g., three-carbon chain with double bond = propene; with triple bond = propyne).
  • Nomenclature Table Examples:
  • Haloalkane: Prefix -chloro, -bromo (e.g., Chloropropane, Bromopropane).
  • Alcohol: Suffix -ol (e.g., Propanol).
  • Aldehyde: Suffix -al (e.g., Propanal).
  • Ketone: Suffix -one (e.g., Propanone).
  • Carboxylic acid: Suffix -oic acid (e.g., Propanoic acid).
  • Alkenes: Suffix -ene (e.g., Propene).
  • Alkynes: Suffix -yne (e.g., Propyne).

    • 4.3 Chemical Properties of Carbon Compounds

    4.3.1 Combustion

  • Process: Carbon, in all its allotropic forms, burns in oxygen to produce carbon dioxide along with heat and light. Most carbon compounds also release significant heat and light upon burning. These are oxidation reactions.
  • C + O₂ → CO₂ + heat and light
  • CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O + heat and light
  • CH₃CH₂OH + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O + heat and light
  • Flame Characteristics:
  • Saturated hydrocarbons generally give a clean flame.
  • Unsaturated carbon compounds typically give a yellow flame with a lot of black smoke (sooty deposit).
  • Incomplete combustion (limiting air supply) of even saturated hydrocarbons can produce a sooty flame.
  • Household gas/kerosene stoves have air inlets for an oxygen-rich mixture, producing a clean blue flame. Blackened cooking vessel bottoms indicate blocked air holes and fuel wastage.
  • Pollutants: Combustion of fuels like coal and petroleum, which contain nitrogen and sulphur, results in the formation of oxides of sulphur and nitrogen, major environmental pollutants.
  • Flame Formation: A flame is produced only when gaseous substances burn. Luminous flames occur when atoms of the gaseous substance are heated and glow. The yellow color of a candle flame is attributed to incomplete combustion, producing soot (carbon).
  • Fossil Fuels: Coal and petroleum are fossil fuels formed from biomass (plants and tiny animals) over millions of years through biological and geological processes.

    • 4.3.2 Oxidation

  • Carbon compounds can be easily oxidized through combustion.
  • Beyond complete oxidation, alcohols can be converted to carboxylic acids.
  • Example: Ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) can be oxidized to Ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH).
  • Oxidising Agents: Substances capable of adding oxygen to others.
  • Examples: Alkaline potassium permanganate or acidified potassium dichromate are used to oxidize alcohols to acids.

    • 4.3.3 Addition Reaction

  • Process: Unsaturated hydrocarbons (containing double or triple bonds) can add hydrogen atoms.
  • Catalysts: This reaction occurs in the presence of catalysts such as palladium or nickel. Catalysts cause a reaction to proceed at a different rate without being affected themselves.
  • Product: The addition of hydrogen converts unsaturated hydrocarbons into saturated hydrocarbons.
  • Industrial Application: This reaction is commonly used in the hydrogenation of vegetable oils using a nickel catalyst.
  • Vegetable oils generally have long unsaturated carbon chains, while animal fats have saturated carbon chains.
  • Oils containing unsaturated fatty acids are considered healthier for cooking.

    • 4.3.4 Substitution Reaction

  • Reactivity: Saturated hydrocarbons are generally unreactive and inert in the presence of most reagents.
  • Process: In the presence of sunlight, elements like chlorine can replace hydrogen atoms in a hydrocarbon. This is called a substitution reaction because one type of atom (or group) takes the place of another.
  • Products: A number of products can be formed, especially with higher homologues of alkanes.
  • Example: CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl (in the presence of sunlight).

    • 4.4 Some Important Carbon Compounds – Ethanol and Ethanoic Acid

    4.4.1 Properties of Ethanol

  • General Information:
  • A liquid at room temperature.
  • Commonly called alcohol, it is the active ingredient in all alcoholic drinks.
  • A good solvent, used in medicines like tincture iodine, cough syrups, and tonics.
  • Soluble in water in all proportions.
  • Consumption of small quantities of dilute ethanol causes drunkenness.
  • Intake of even small quantities of pure ethanol (absolute alcohol) can be lethal. Long-term consumption leads to various health problems.
  • Methanol (CH₃OH) is highly poisonous; even small quantities can cause death. In the liver, methanol is oxidized to methanal, which rapidly reacts with cell components, coagulating protoplasm and affecting the optic nerve, leading to blindness.
  • Denatured alcohol is industrial ethanol made unfit for drinking by adding poisonous substances like methanol and dyes (to color it blue) to prevent misuse.
  • Reactions of Ethanol:
  • Reaction with Sodium: Alcohols react with sodium to evolve hydrogen gas. With ethanol, the other product is sodium ethoxide.
  • 2Na + 2CH₃CH₂OH → 2CH₃CH₂O⁻Na⁺ (Sodium ethoxide) + H₂
  • Reaction to give Unsaturated Hydrocarbon (Dehydration): Heating ethanol at 443 K with excess concentrated sulphuric acid causes dehydration, producing ethene.
  • CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O (in the presence of Hot Conc. H₂SO₄ at 443 K)
  • Concentrated sulphuric acid acts as a dehydrating agent.
  • Alcohol as a Fuel:
  • Ethanol can be produced by fermenting molasses (from sugarcane juice).
  • Some countries use alcohol as an additive in petrol because it is a cleaner fuel, producing only carbon dioxide and water when burned in sufficient air.

    • 4.4.2 Properties of Ethanoic Acid

  • General Information:
  • Commonly called acetic acid, belonging to the group of carboxylic acids.
  • A 5-8% solution of acetic acid in water is called vinegar, widely used as a preservative in pickles.
  • The melting point of pure ethanoic acid is 290 K, causing it to often freeze in cold winters, earning it the name glacial acetic acid.
  • Carboxylic acids are characterized by their acidic nature, but they are weak acids compared to mineral acids like HCl, which are completely ionized.
  • Reactions of Ethanoic Acid:
  • Esterification Reaction: Ethanoic acid reacts with absolute ethanol (an alcohol) in the presence of an acid catalyst to produce an ester and water.
  • CH₃COOH (Ethanoic acid) + CH₃CH₂OH (Ethanol) → CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ (Ester) + H₂O (in presence of Acid)
  • Esters are typically sweet-smelling substances used in perfumes and as flavouring agents.
  • Saponification: Treating an ester with sodium hydroxide (an alkali) converts it back to alcohol and the sodium salt of a carboxylic acid. This reaction is called saponification because it's used in making soap(sodium or potassium salts of long-chain carboxylic acids).
  • CH₃COOC₂H₅ + NaOH → C₂H₅OH + CH₃COONa
  • Reaction with a Base: Like mineral acids, ethanoic acid reacts with a base (e.g., sodium hydroxide) to form a salt (sodium ethanoate/acetate) and water.
  • NaOH + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O
  • Reaction with Carbonates and Hydrogencarbonates: Ethanoic acid reacts with carbonates (e.g., sodium carbonate) and hydrogencarbonates (e.g., sodium hydrogencarbonate) to produce a salt (sodium acetate), carbon dioxide, and water.
  • 2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂
  • CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂

    • 4.5 Soaps and Detergents

  • Cleaning Action of Soaps:
  • Most dirt is oily and does not dissolve in water.
  • Soap molecules are sodium or potassium salts of long-chain carboxylic acids.
  • They have two distinct ends:
  • An ionic-end (hydrophilic) which interacts with water.
  • A carbon chain (hydrophobic) which interacts with oil/hydrocarbons.
  • When soap is in water, the hydrophobic tails are in the interior of the cluster, and the hydrophilic ionic ends are on the surface. This unique orientation forms structures called micelles.
  • The soap micelle helps trap oily dirt in its center, forming an emulsion in water.
  • The micelles, with the dirt suspended within them, stay in solution as a colloid and do not precipitate due to ion-ion repulsion, allowing the dirt to be easily rinsed away.
  • Soap solutions appear cloudy because the micelles are large enough to scatter light.
  • Hard Water and Scum Formation:
  • Hard water contains calcium and magnesium salts.
  • When soap reacts with these salts, it forms an insoluble substance called scum (a white curdy precipitate).
  • This reaction reduces the effectiveness of soap, requiring a larger amount of soap to be used.
  • Detergents:
  • Detergents are a class of cleansing agents developed to overcome the problem of scum formation in hard water.
  • They are generally sodium salts of sulphonic acids or ammonium salts with chloride or bromide ions.
  • Like soaps, they have long hydrocarbon chains.
  • The charged ends of detergents do not form insoluble precipitates with calcium and magnesium ions present in hard water.
  • Therefore, detergents remain effective in hard water.
  • Detergents are commonly used in shampoos and products for cleaning clothes.
